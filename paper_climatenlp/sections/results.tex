\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate \sys{} along four axes: a synthetic flaw-injection protocol, an
architecture ablation, a run over 27 published difference-in-differences (DID)
papers that stress-test the audit architecture across empirical economics, beyond
the climate-policy setting (carbon pricing, emissions trading, emission standards)
that defines its target deployment, and a calibrated human-gold pilot. Four findings emerge: (i)~judging evidence \emph{adequacy},
not keyword presence, is what detects identification flaws; (ii)~restructuring
the same model into a bounded per-dimension pipeline recovers flaws a single pass
misses and turns retrieval failure into an auditable \texttt{unknown} signal;
(iii)~on real papers the dominant practical bottleneck shifts from causal
reasoning to evidence grounding;
and (iv)~against a human gold \sys{} is systematically over-severe, a bias that a
lightweight calibration layer reduces without lowering high-risk detection on the
pilot's few high cells.

\subsection{Adequacy beats presence; architecture beats a single pass}
\label{sec:ablation}

We compare three assessors under one flaw-injection protocol on the
\texttt{clean\_supported} fixture (one flaw injected per run, all eleven flaws;
$\mathrm{max\_steps}{=}1$). A deterministic \textbf{keyword baseline} scores each
dimension from the presence of supporting and risk-indicating vocabulary. The
\textbf{two-stage \sys{} assessor} judges evidence \emph{adequacy} (whether the
reported evidence actually establishes the assumption) over the \emph{same
retrieved evidence} as the keyword baseline, so that pair isolates the judgement
policy. A \textbf{single-pass} ablation removes the architecture entirely: the
full paper and all eleven dimensions in one \texttt{gpt-4o} call, with the same
model, temperature, risk schema, and scoring harness, but no staged retrieval,
relevance gating, bounded tool use, or explicit \texttt{unknown} state. The only
difference is architectural. Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the headline metrics.

\paragraph{The keyword baseline is blind to commission flaws.}
The baseline never raises a false alarm and correctly flags the two omission
flaws (\texttt{measurement\_break}, \texttt{spillover\_contamination}) by the
\emph{absence} of their vocabulary, but beyond those detection is only $0.182$
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}B). Two failure modes explain it: \emph{cross-section
vocabulary bleed} (deleting the placebo section does not help when another
section also mentions ``placebo'') and blindness to \emph{every} commission
flaw, whose flawed-but-plausible evidence carries the expected topic vocabulary. Keyword presence is not evidence adequacy.

\paragraph{Reasoning over adequacy closes the gap.}
Swapping \emph{only} the assessment policy (extraction unchanged, so both see the
same evidence) to an LLM that judges adequacy raises detection from $0.182$ to
$1.000$ with no false alarms and $0.909$ localization. The model reads
flawed-but-natural prose the keyword scorer cannot: for the injected anticipation
flaw it returns \emph{high\,/\,flawed}, reasoning that ``outcomes began changing
between the policy announcement and rollout,'' with no sentinel keyword present.
This is the empirical core of the design: placing agency where reasoning is
required.

\paragraph{The same model, restructured, recovers what a single pass misses.}
The single-pass ablation holds the model fixed and removes the architecture. On the flaw-injection
benchmark it detects only $0.455$ of flaws (false alarm $0.000$, localization
$0.455$), against $1.000$/$0.000$/$0.909$ for the two-stage assessor: judging
eleven dimensions from the whole paper at once misses more than half the planted
flaws that per-dimension retrieval isolates. On the 25 papers common to both runs
($275$ cells) the contrast sharpens. Single-pass almost never abstains: its
\texttt{unknown} share is $0.4\%$ versus $38.9\%$ for the two-stage pipeline. The
cells the bounded pipeline marks \texttt{unknown} become confident judgements
under single-pass, mostly \emph{low} ($1.8\%\!\to\!14.9\%$) or \emph{medium}
($14.2\%\!\to\!43.6\%$), while the high-risk share is comparable ($41.1\%$ vs
$45.1\%$). Without a gate the model commits a substantive risk for nearly every
dimension instead of reporting that it could not ground the evidence. The bounded
design thus recovers flaws a single pass misses and turns retrieval failure into a
visible, auditable signal: the distinction the rest of the section relies on. One
caveat: the two-stage pipeline also issues two model calls per dimension against
one call in total for single-pass, so decomposition and per-call compute are not
separated by this comparison; a compute-matched ablation is future work.

\paragraph{Scope.}
These are synthetic fixtures with few dimensions; $1.000$ is a proof-of-concept on
clearly injected flaws, not a solved task. The sharper tests follow: subtler flaws
(\S\ref{sec:variants}), then real papers (\S\ref{sec:realpapers}).

\subsection{Robustness: a larger, harder variant benchmark}
\label{sec:variants}
The eleven flaws above are deliberately clear-cut. To test whether the result
survives subtler and more varied flaws, we built a second benchmark of 33 flaw
variants: three per dimension (two \emph{commission} rewrites and one
\emph{omission}), each verified to contain none of the keyword detector's sentinel
phrases. We audit this set with the two-stage \sys{} assessor (gpt-4o) over three
runs at temperature~$0$; verdicts are near-deterministic (32/33 identical across
runs). Detection is $0.73$--$0.76$ and localization $0.64$--$0.70$, below the
saturated $1.000$ on the eleven clear flaws.

The split by flaw type is the informative result (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). The
assessor catches $0.86$--$0.91$ of \emph{commission} flaws (flawed-but-present
evidence, the case adequacy reasoning targets) but only $0.45$ of \emph{omission}
flaws. The omission misses are not confident errors: the six missed variants all
abstain to \texttt{unknown} rather than assert low risk, because deleting the
supporting section makes retrieval fail. This reproduces, under controlled
injection, the same \emph{evidence-grounding} bottleneck we find on real papers
(\S\ref{sec:realpapers}): the pipeline reasons well over evidence it can retrieve
and abstains when it cannot. The only false-alarm signal ($0.09$, identical across
runs) is concentrated in \emph{inference}, where the clean fixture's section is
itself flagged, the same over-severity we quantify against the human gold
(\S\ref{sec:vsgold}). The ``no false alarms'' result in Table~\ref{tab:results} is
on the original, clearer eleven-flaw set; this harder benchmark surfaces the
inference over-flag.

\subsection{From synthetic fixtures to real papers: a relocated bottleneck}
\label{sec:realpapers}

We next audit a corpus of 27 published DID papers. There is no injected ground
truth; we report
the raw per-dimension risk distribution of each assessor
(Figure~\ref{fig:realcorpus}). The keyword baseline is permissive: $91\%$ of its
judgements are \emph{low}. It sees topic vocabulary, declares the dimension
supported, and flags almost nothing. The two-stage LLM is differentiated, and its
most informative output is that ${\sim}39\%$ of judgements are \texttt{unknown}:
the relevance gate could not surface evidence, so the system says so rather than
over-flagging. Two regimes appear: dimensions whose discussion is diffuse or
lives in figures the text retrieval cannot reach (parallel trends, no
anticipation, placebo) are dominated by \texttt{unknown}, while dimensions where a
section is retrieved (sample period, concurrent policies, SUTVA) are judged
inadequate. The finding is methodological: moving to real papers is bottlenecked
not by causal reasoning but by \emph{evidence grounding}. The design therefore
reports retrieval quality and an explicit \texttt{unknown} state distinct from a
substantive high-risk judgement.

Judgements are grounded rather than pattern-matched
(Appendix~\ref{app:qual} gives a qualitative example). Roughly $45\%$ of
judgements on these top-journal papers are \emph{high}; without dimension-level
expert labels we cannot yet say whether this is warranted or over-strict where
retrieval succeeds. We claim the contrast in behaviour and the localization of the
grounding bottleneck, not the correctness of any individual flag.

\subsection{A calibrated human gold (pilot)}
\label{sec:gold}

To test whether \sys{}'s flags agree with expert judgement, we built a small
expert gold: two annotators independently labelled risk on 5 DID papers $\times$
11 dimensions (55 cells). Round-1 agreement was low on the raw 3-level scale
(Cohen's $\kappa=0.17$), but the disagreement is almost entirely ordinal
(weighted $\kappa=0.31$; $96\%$ of disagreements are a single severity step, so
annotators almost never reverse direction). A calibration round adjudicated the 27
disagreements: 16 resolved to a single label once the low/medium boundary was
anchored, and 11 were flagged intrinsically ambiguous. Those ambiguous cells are
not random: they concentrate on \emph{non-canonical DID designs}
(continuous-shock, event-study) where a dimension like staggered timing is not
applicable as conventionally defined. A real share of ``expert disagreement'' is
thus \emph{design applicability}, not severity; on those cells even experts
abstain. The adjudicated gold (55 cells) is low 29, medium 24, high 2. This is a
pilot ($n{=}5$, two annotators, one adjudication round), read as preliminary.

\subsection{\sys{} versus the human gold}
\label{sec:vsgold}

We use this gold as a \emph{diagnostic pilot}, not a validation of accuracy: with
only two high-risk gold cells across five papers it cannot establish agreement
with experts, but it is enough to localize \emph{how} \sys{} errs
(Table~\ref{tab:vsgold}, Figure~\ref{fig:vsgold}). The errors are systematic, not
random. \sys{} abstains on 22/55 cells, and when it answers its errors are almost
entirely one-directional: 28/33 answered cells are more severe than the
adjudicated gold, and none are less severe. Human gold contains only two
high-risk cells, whereas \sys{} assigns high risk to 24. Because the pilot gold
has so few high labels, high precision and recall are noisy; the robust
conclusion is that the current assessor is over-sensitive to missing or partial
evidence and is best read as a \emph{high-sensitivity screening tool} for
prioritizing human review.

\subsection{Calibration: from diagnosis to improvement}
\label{sec:calib}

The gold comparison also points to a fix. An error analysis of the over-severe
cells is sharp: of \sys{}'s over-severe \emph{high} judgements, $23/24$ have
\texttt{evidence\_status=missing} and $20/24$ have
\texttt{retrieval\_quality=weak}. \sys{} treats ``weak retrieval found nothing''
as a substantive \emph{high}, conflating a \emph{retrieval failure} with the paper
lacking the evidence, which the human, reading the full PDF, resolves to
low/medium. This is a fixable calibration target.

A deterministic calibration layer over fields the LLM already produces cuts
over-severity while holding high-risk detection unchanged on the pilot's two high
cells; the dominant rule demotes a weak-retrieval \emph{high} to \emph{medium}
(keep coverage) or \texttt{unknown} (abstain). Keeping coverage
raises exact agreement from $0.15$ to
$0.45$; abstaining reaches weighted $\kappa=0.25$ but leaves 13 answered cells
(Appendix, Table~\ref{tab:calib}, Figure~\ref{fig:calib_tradeoff}). Calibration
corrects severity but cannot recover missing evidence (correct \emph{coverage}
needs better grounding), so the human gold does more than evaluate \sys{}; it
calibrates it.
