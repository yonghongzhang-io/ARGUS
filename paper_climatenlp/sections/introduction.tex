\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Difference-in-differences (DID) is widely used to evaluate climate and
environmental policy, including carbon pricing, emissions-trading schemes, and
emission standards. The credibility of these estimates depends on identification
assumptions such as parallel trends, no anticipation, appropriate treatment of
staggered adoption, and limited interference between units, each of which a decade
of methodological work has shown to be fragile
\cite{bertrand2004trust,goodmanbacon2021,callaway2021,sunabraham2021,dechaisemartin2020,roth2023}.
These assumptions are often difficult to assess in climate settings, where policies overlap,
implementation is staggered, spillovers cross administrative boundaries, and
emissions outcomes are imperfectly measured (Appendix~\ref{app:motivation}).
Because environmental policy increasingly rests on this empirical evidence,
causal conclusions built on weakly supported assumptions carry real costs for
climate governance.

Yet applied papers often assert these assumptions rather than audit whether the
reported evidence supports them, one facet of a broader reliability gap in
empirical research \cite{ioannidis2005,silberzahn2018many,brodeur2020methods}. A paper may state that parallel trends hold after
presenting an event-study figure, while leaving the reader to determine whether the
design, pre-period estimates, inference, and policy timing actually justify that
claim. Performing this assessment manually is slow, expert-intensive, and difficult
to standardize.

We argue that identification credibility is auditable even when the true causal
effect is not, building on the research-design tradition in applied econometrics
\cite{angristpischke2010,atheyimbens2017,rambachanroth2023}. Because the
counterfactual is unobserved, \sys{} does not ask whether an estimated effect is
true. Instead, it asks whether each identifying
assumption is adequately supported by the evidence reported in the paper, and where
that support is weakest. \sys{} (named for Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed
watchman of Greek myth) represents a DID study through an 11-dimension
\emph{assumption--implication--evidence} rubric and uses a bounded-agentic pipeline
to retrieve relevant evidence, assess its adequacy, and produce an evidence-grounded
risk report for expert review (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Model agency is confined
to scoped retrieval and assessment tasks behind a fixed, deterministic control flow,
unlike free-roaming agents whose control flow is model-decided \cite{yao2023react};
\sys{} flags potential weaknesses rather than adjudicating causal truth.

Evaluating such an auditor is difficult because the true policy effect is
unobserved. We therefore use \emph{flaw injection} as local ground truth: starting
from a supported study fixture, we introduce one known identification flaw and test
whether \sys{} detects it, remains quiet on the clean version, and localizes the
affected dimension. We complement this controlled evaluation with deployment on 27
real DID papers and a small expert-labelled diagnostic pilot used to identify
calibration and applicability failures.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item an 11-dimension framework that operationalizes DID identification
  credibility as machine-auditable \emph{assumption--implication--evidence}
  chains (\S\ref{sec:framework});
  \item a bounded-agentic, evidence-grounded auditing pipeline that separates
  retrieval uncertainty from substantive identification risk;
  \item a layered evaluation combining realistic flaw injection, same-model
  architectural ablation, real-paper deployment, and a diagnostic
  expert-calibration pilot (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}
