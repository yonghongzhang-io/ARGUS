\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The right target for AI assistance in empirical climate-policy research is not the
unobservable causal effect but the \emph{identification credibility} of the study
that estimates it. \sys{} operationalizes this stance: a bounded-agentic pipeline
decomposes identification into auditable dimensions, gathers and assesses in-paper
evidence, and localizes risk in a transparent, human-reviewable report, while
confining model agency to two scoped, traceable sub-tasks. We evaluate such
auditors with synthetic flaw injection, which supplies reproducible local ground
truth without access to the true effect. Once the leakage that made this evaluation
circular is removed, a keyword baseline is blind to flawed-but-plausible evidence
($0.182$ detection), while a model that judges evidence adequacy recovers it
($1.000$, no false alarms). A human-gold diagnostic pilot further shows that expert
labels can do more than evaluate the auditor: they expose a systematic
over-severity bias and calibrate the mapping from evidence gaps to audit risk.
Scaling this from synthetic fixtures to a real, expert-annotated corpus with humans
in the loop remains the central open challenge. Auditing identification credibility
is, we argue, both a tractable and a responsible role for AI in empirical
environmental-policy research.
