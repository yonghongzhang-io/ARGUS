\begin{abstract}
Difference-in-differences (DID) designs underpin much of the empirical evidence
on climate policy, yet their identification assumptions are more often asserted
than audited. Because the counterfactual is never observed, we ask instead
whether the evidence a study reports adequately supports each assumption. We
present \sys{}, which scores a DID study on an eleven-dimension
assumption--implication--evidence rubric: a language model retrieves and
adjudicates evidence behind deterministic control flow, and abstains on any
dimension a relevance gate cannot ground in the text. Under controlled flaw
injection, an LLM pipeline that judges evidence adequacy detects $0.73$ of planted
flaws against $0.18$ for a keyword-presence pipeline, with false alarms no higher
than $0.09$. On published economics
papers the bottleneck shifts to evidence grounding. The system abstains on
$40\%$ of dimensions; where it does answer, it is more severe than the experts
on 28 of 33 pilot cells. One gate explains both. A short fixture leaves nothing
to retrieve, so the system defers; a full paper always turns up a partial match,
which it judges rather than defers. A deterministic rule that demotes those
ungrounded judgements raises exact agreement from $0.12$ to $0.36$ on the pilot
it was derived from. \sys{} is a screening instrument:
it localizes weakly evidenced assumptions for expert review rather than
adjudicating causal claims.
\end{abstract}
