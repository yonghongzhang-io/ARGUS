\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Problem: identification assumptions in DID policy studies are often asserted, not audited.
Difference-in-differences (DID) is the workhorse design for evaluating
environmental policy---carbon pricing, emissions-trading pilots, industrial
standards. Its credibility rests on identification assumptions (parallel trends,
no anticipation, correctly handled staggered timing, no interfering spillovers)
that are frequently \emph{asserted} rather than \emph{audited}, even as the
methodological literature has shown how fragile each can be
\cite{goodmanbacon2021,callaway2021,sunabraham2021,roth2023}.

% Our stance: audit identification credibility, NOT the true causal effect.
We argue identification credibility is \emph{auditable} even though the true
causal effect is not. The counterfactual is never observed, so we do not ask
``is the estimated effect true?''. Instead we ask whether each identification
assumption is \emph{supported by the evidence the paper itself reports}, and we
\emph{localize} where support is weak. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} sketches this:
identification credibility varies across assumptions, evidence, and policy
context, and ARGUS audits it by decomposition and evidence-grounded risk flags.

% Contributions (mirror paper/contributions.md)
\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~\textbf{ARGUS}, a bounded-agentic framework that audits identification
credibility---not causal truth---in DID environmental-policy evaluation;
(2)~an identification rubric of 11 auditable dimensions plus a flaw taxonomy;
(3)~a flaw-injection methodology giving reproducible \emph{local ground truth}
without the true effect; and (4)~an honest deterministic baseline and a negative
result that quantifies the reasoning gap this PhD targets (\S\ref{sec:results}).
