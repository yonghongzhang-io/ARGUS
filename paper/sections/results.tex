\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:results}

We compare two assessment policies under the same flaw-injection protocol and the
\emph{same retrieved evidence} (extraction is identical), so the comparison
isolates how each judges that evidence. The first is a deterministic, non-LLM
\textbf{keyword baseline}: it scores each dimension from the presence or absence
of supporting and risk-indicating vocabulary. The second is an
\textbf{LLM assessor} that judges evidence \emph{adequacy}---whether the reported
evidence actually establishes the assumption. We run both over all eleven flaws on
the \texttt{clean\_supported} fixture, injecting one flaw per run.
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the headline metrics; the per-flaw breakdown is in
\texttt{experiments/phase1\_pilot/}, and Figure~\ref{fig:results} is generated
from these runs.

\paragraph{The keyword baseline is blind to commission flaws.}
The baseline never raises a false alarm, and it correctly flags the two omission
flaws---\texttt{measurement\_break} and \texttt{spillover\_contamination}---whose
vocabulary does not appear elsewhere in the paper, detecting them by the
\emph{absence} of evidence. But beyond those, detection is only $0.182$
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}, panel~B). Two failure modes explain it: \emph{cross-section
vocabulary bleed}, where deleting the placebo section does not help because another
section also mentions ``placebo''; and, more fundamentally, blindness to
\emph{every} commission flaw, where flawed-but-plausible evidence is present and
uses the expected topic vocabulary. Keyword presence is not evidence adequacy.

\paragraph{Reasoning over adequacy closes the gap.}
Swapping \emph{only} the assessment policy---extraction is unchanged, so both see
the same evidence---to an LLM that judges adequacy raises detection from $0.182$ to
$1.000$ with no false alarms and $0.909$ localization. The model reads the
flawed-but-natural prose the keyword scorer cannot: for the injected anticipation
flaw it returns \emph{high / flawed} with the rationale that ``outcomes began
changing between the policy announcement and rollout,'' even though no sentinel
keyword is present. This is the empirical core of the project---evidence
\emph{presence} versus \emph{adequacy}---and it validates the bounded-agentic
design: agency placed exactly where reasoning is required.

\paragraph{Scope.}
These are synthetic fixtures with a small number of dimensions; $1.000$ is a
proof-of-concept on clearly injected flaws, not a claim of a solved task. The
sharper question is whether the result survives on real published papers, to
which we now turn.

\subsection{From synthetic fixtures to real papers}
\label{sec:realpapers}
We next audit 27 published DID papers from the CausalVerify corpus~\cite{causalverify_dataset}---%
empirical economics broadly, with environmental policy as the motivating case.
Here there is no injected ground truth; we report the raw per-dimension risk
distribution of each assessor (Figure~\ref{fig:realcorpus}).

\paragraph{Two regimes, and a relocated bottleneck.}
The keyword baseline is credulous: $91\%$ of its judgements are \emph{low}---on
real papers it sees topic vocabulary and declares the dimension supported,
flagging almost nothing. The two-stage LLM is differentiated, but its most
informative output is that $\sim$$39\%$ of judgements are \texttt{unknown}: the
relevance gate could not surface evidence for that dimension, so the system says
so rather than over-flagging. Two regimes appear---dimensions whose discussion is
diffuse or lives in figures the text retrieval cannot reach (parallel trends, no
anticipation, placebo) are dominated by \texttt{unknown}, while dimensions where a
section is retrieved (sample period, concurrent policies, SUTVA) are judged
inadequate. The central finding is methodological: moving from synthetic fixtures
to real papers is bottlenecked not by causal reasoning but by \emph{evidence
grounding}, which is exactly why the two-stage design reports retrieval quality
and an explicit \texttt{unknown} state distinct from a substantive high-risk
judgement (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{What the assessor actually reads.}
The judgements are grounded in the retrieved text, not pattern-matched. On a
trade-and-wages paper ARGUS retrieves the employment-results section and flags
\emph{inference} as high because ``the paper only mentions heteroskedastic-%
consistent standard errors and does not provide information on clustering or the
number of clusters, which is crucial for the dependence structure in panel
data''---a specific econometric observation, not a keyword hit. On another paper
it retrieves an ``Alternative Market Share Specification'' section and flags
\emph{robustness/placebo} as high, noting that this ``only discusses an
alternative specification'' and is not a placebo or falsification test. In both
cases the model distinguishes a \emph{related but different} reported check from
the one the assumption actually requires---presence versus adequacy, now on real
papers.

\paragraph{What we do not yet claim.}
Roughly $45\%$ of the LLM's judgements on these top-journal papers are
\emph{high}; without dimension-level expert labels we cannot say whether this is
warranted or over-strict where retrieval succeeds. Calibrating ARGUS against
expert annotation on this corpus is the immediate next step
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}); the defensible claim here is the contrast in behaviour
and the localization of the grounding bottleneck, not the correctness of any
individual flag.

\subsection{A calibrated human gold (pilot)}
\label{sec:gold}
To ask whether ARGUS's flags are \emph{right}, not just different, we built a
small expert gold: two annotators independently labelled risk on 5 DID papers
$\times$ 11 dimensions (55 cells). Round-1 agreement was low on the raw 3-level
scale (Cohen's $\kappa=0.17$) but the disagreement is almost entirely ordinal:
weighted $\kappa=0.31$ and \textbf{96\% of disagreements are a single severity
step}---annotators essentially never reverse direction. A calibration round then
adjudicated the 27 disagreements: \textbf{16 resolved} to a single label once the
low/medium boundary was anchored, and \textbf{11 were flagged intrinsically
ambiguous}. Those ambiguous cells are not random---they concentrate on
\emph{non-canonical DID designs} (continuous-shock, event-study) where a dimension
like staggered timing is \emph{not applicable as conventionally defined}. A real
share of ``expert disagreement'' is thus \emph{design applicability}, not severity,
which is itself why even experts abstain. The adjudicated gold (55 cells) is low
29, medium 24, \textbf{high 2}. This is a pilot ($n{=}5$, two annotators, one
adjudication round); we read it as indicative, not definitive.

\subsection{ARGUS vs the human gold}
\label{sec:vsgold}
Comparing ARGUS-LLM's risk to this gold (Table~\ref{tab:vsgold}) gives the first
calibration signal---and it is unambiguous. ARGUS answers 33/55 cells and abstains
\texttt{unknown} on 22. On the answered cells it agrees only weakly (weighted
$\kappa=0.04$), and the failure is one-directional: ARGUS is more severe than the
gold in \textbf{28 of 33} answered cells and \emph{less} severe in none. It emits
\emph{high} on 24/55 cells where the gold has high on only 2---high precision
$0.04$ (1/24) at recall $0.50$. The gold's near-absence of \emph{high} against
ARGUS's $\sim$45\% high on the broader corpus is the core gap.

\paragraph{Reading: a screening tool, not a judge.}
The current ARGUS-LLM is not an autonomous adjudicator---when it commits, it
systematically over-flags severity. The constructive reading is that ARGUS is
better cast as a \emph{high-sensitivity screening tool} that prioritizes
dimensions for human review, which is exactly the ``flag, not judge'' stance: the
\texttt{unknown} abstention channel should stay separate, and substantive
\emph{high} needs a calibration / thresholding layer against human anchors.

\subsection{Calibration: from diagnosis to improvement}
\label{sec:calib}
The gold comparison is diagnostic, not just evaluative. An error analysis of the
over-severe cells is sharp: of ARGUS's over-severe \emph{high} judgements,
$23/24$ have \texttt{evidence\_status=missing} and $20/24$ have
\texttt{retrieval\_quality=weak}. ARGUS treats ``weak retrieval found nothing'' as
a substantive \emph{high}, conflating a \emph{retrieval failure} with the paper
lacking the evidence---precisely what the human, reading the full PDF, resolves to
low/medium. This is a calibration target, not random error.

We add a deterministic calibration layer over the LLM output, keyed on the rich
fields it already produces: the dominant rule converts a weak-retrieval
\emph{high} (no contradiction signalled in the rationale) either to \emph{medium}
(keep coverage) or to \texttt{unknown} (abstain); smaller rules soften a few
anachronistic or reporting-gap \emph{high}s. Against the gold
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}), both policies cut over-severity and \textbf{preserve
high-risk recall ($0.50$)}---severity is reduced without losing detection. The
keep-coverage policy keeps all 33 answers and raises exact agreement from $0.12$
to $0.46$; the abstain policy reaches the highest weighted $\kappa$ ($0.25$) but
collapses coverage to 13 answered cells. Neither addresses the root cause---weak
retrieval---so the lift that would add \emph{correct} coverage must come from
better evidence grounding. The methodological point stands: \textbf{the human
gold does not only evaluate ARGUS---it calibrates it}.
