\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:results}

We compare three assessors under the same flaw-injection protocol on the
\texttt{clean\_supported} fixture (one flaw injected per run, all eleven flaws). A
deterministic, non-LLM \textbf{keyword baseline} scores each dimension from the
presence or absence of supporting and risk-indicating vocabulary. The
\textbf{two-stage ARGUS assessor} judges evidence \emph{adequacy}---whether the
reported evidence actually establishes the assumption---over the \emph{same
retrieved evidence} as the keyword baseline (extraction is identical), so that
pair isolates the judgement policy. A \textbf{single-pass} ablation removes the
architecture entirely (whole paper and all dimensions in one call;
\S\ref{sec:ablation}). Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the headline metrics; the
per-flaw breakdown is in \texttt{experiments/phase1\_pilot/}, and
Figure~\ref{fig:results} is generated from these runs.

\paragraph{The keyword baseline is blind to commission flaws.}
The baseline never raises a false alarm, and it correctly flags the two omission
flaws (\texttt{measurement\_break} and \texttt{spillover\_contamination}), whose
vocabulary does not appear elsewhere in the paper, detecting them by the
\emph{absence} of evidence. But beyond those, detection is only $0.182$
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}, panel~B). Two failure modes explain it: \emph{cross-section
vocabulary bleed}, where deleting the placebo section does not help because another
section also mentions ``placebo''; and, more fundamentally, blindness to
\emph{every} commission flaw, where flawed-but-plausible evidence is present and
uses the expected topic vocabulary. Keyword presence is not evidence adequacy.

\paragraph{Reasoning over adequacy closes the gap.}
Swapping \emph{only} the assessment policy---extraction is unchanged, so both see
the same evidence---to an LLM that judges adequacy raises detection from $0.182$ to
$1.000$ with no false alarms and $0.909$ localization. The model reads the
flawed-but-natural prose the keyword scorer cannot: for the injected anticipation
flaw it returns \emph{high / flawed} with the rationale that ``outcomes began
changing between the policy announcement and rollout,'' even though no sentinel
keyword is present. This distinction is the empirical
core of the project, and it validates the bounded-agentic design of placing
agency where reasoning is required.

\paragraph{Scope.}
These are synthetic fixtures with a small number of dimensions; $1.000$ is a
proof-of-concept on clearly injected flaws, not a claim of a solved task. The
sharper question is whether the result survives on real published papers
(\S\ref{sec:realpapers}); we first ask what the bounded architecture itself
contributes.

\subsection{Architecture ablation: single-pass prompting versus bounded auditing}
\label{sec:ablation}
The results above show that an LLM-based assessor can reason about reported
evidence adequacy beyond keyword matching, but they do not isolate the
contribution of the bounded ARGUS architecture. We therefore add a single-pass
ablation: the baseline receives the full paper and the same eleven-dimension
rubric in one model call, using the same provider, model (\texttt{gpt-4o}),
temperature, risk schema, and scoring harness, but removing staged retrieval,
relevance gating, bounded tool use, and the explicit evidence-grounding
(\texttt{unknown}) state. The only difference is architectural.

\paragraph{Synthetic flaws.}
On the flaw-injection benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:results}) the single-pass assessor
detects only $0.455$ of injected flaws (false alarm $0.000$, localization
$0.455$), against $1.000$, $0.000$, and $0.909$ for the bounded two-stage assessor. Judging eleven
dimensions from the whole paper at once, the model misses more than half of the
planted flaws that per-dimension retrieval and assessment isolate. The
architecture therefore contributes to detection itself, not only to how evidence
is presented.

\paragraph{Real papers.}
On the 25 papers common to both runs ($275$ cells) the contrast is sharper. The
single-pass baseline almost never abstains: its \texttt{unknown} share is $0.4\%$,
versus $38.9\%$ for the two-stage pipeline. The cells the bounded pipeline marks
\texttt{unknown}---where the relevance gate could not surface evidence---become
confident judgements under single-pass, mostly \emph{low} ($1.8\%\to14.9\%$) or
\emph{medium} ($14.2\%\to43.6\%$), while the high-risk share is comparable
($41.1\%$ vs $45.1\%$). Without a gate the model commits a substantive risk for
nearly every dimension instead of reporting that it could not ground the evidence.

\paragraph{What the architecture adds.}
The ablation thus attributes a concrete role to the bounded design beyond raw
model capability: per-dimension retrieval and assessment recover flaws a single
pass misses, and the explicit \texttt{unknown} state turns retrieval failure into
a visible, auditable signal rather than a confident guess. Separating evidence
retrieval from adequacy assessment is what lets ARGUS distinguish substantive
identification risk from a grounding failure---the distinction the rest of
\S\ref{sec:results} relies on.

\subsection{From synthetic fixtures to real papers: diagnosing the grounding
bottleneck}
\label{sec:realpapers}
We next audit 27 published DID papers from the CausalVerify corpus~\cite{causalverify_dataset}---%
empirical economics broadly, with environmental policy as the motivating case.
Here there is no injected ground truth; we report the raw per-dimension risk
distribution of each assessor (Figure~\ref{fig:realcorpus}).

\paragraph{A relocated bottleneck on real papers.}
The keyword baseline is permissive: $91\%$ of its judgements are \emph{low}. On
real papers it sees topic vocabulary, declares the dimension supported, and flags
almost nothing. The two-stage LLM is differentiated, but its most
informative output is that $\sim$$39\%$ of judgements are \texttt{unknown}: the
relevance gate could not surface evidence for that dimension, so the system says
so rather than over-flagging. Two regimes appear---dimensions whose discussion is
diffuse or lives in figures the text retrieval cannot reach (parallel trends, no
anticipation, placebo) are dominated by \texttt{unknown}, while dimensions where a
section is retrieved (sample period, concurrent policies, SUTVA) are judged
inadequate. The finding here is methodological: moving from synthetic fixtures to
real papers is bottlenecked not by causal reasoning but by \emph{evidence
grounding}. This is why the two-stage design reports retrieval quality and an
explicit \texttt{unknown} state, distinct from a substantive high-risk judgement
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{How the assessor judges evidence.}
The judgements are grounded in the retrieved text, not pattern-matched. On a
trade-and-wages paper ARGUS retrieves the employment-results section and flags
\emph{inference} as high because ``the paper only mentions heteroskedastic-%
consistent standard errors and does not provide information on clustering or the
number of clusters, which is crucial for the dependence structure in panel
data''---a specific econometric observation, not a keyword hit. On another paper
it retrieves an ``Alternative Market Share Specification'' section and flags
\emph{robustness/placebo} as high, noting that this ``only discusses an
alternative specification'' and is not a placebo or falsification test. In both
cases the model distinguishes a \emph{related but different} reported check from
the one the assumption actually requires---now on real papers rather than
synthetic fixtures.

\paragraph{Limitations of the real-paper runs.}
Roughly $45\%$ of the LLM's judgements on these top-journal papers are
\emph{high}; without dimension-level expert labels we cannot say whether this is
warranted or over-strict where retrieval succeeds. Calibrating ARGUS against
expert annotation on this corpus is the immediate next step
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}); what we claim is the contrast in behaviour
and the localization of the grounding bottleneck, not the correctness of any
individual flag.

\subsection{A calibrated human gold (pilot)}
\label{sec:gold}
To test whether ARGUS's flags agree with expert judgement, we built a
small expert gold: two annotators independently labelled risk on 5 DID papers
$\times$ 11 dimensions (55 cells). Round-1 agreement was low on the raw 3-level
scale (Cohen's $\kappa=0.17$) but the disagreement is almost entirely ordinal:
weighted $\kappa=0.31$, and 96\% of disagreements are a single severity step, so
annotators almost never reverse direction. A calibration round then
adjudicated the 27 disagreements: 16 resolved to a single label once the
low/medium boundary was anchored, and 11 were flagged as intrinsically ambiguous.
Those ambiguous cells are not random: they concentrate on \emph{non-canonical DID
designs} (continuous-shock, event-study) where a dimension like staggered timing
is not applicable as conventionally defined. A real
share of ``expert disagreement'' is thus \emph{design applicability}, not severity,
which is itself why even experts abstain. The adjudicated gold (55 cells) is low
29, medium 24, high 2. This is a pilot ($n{=}5$, two annotators, one
adjudication round), and we read it as preliminary.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The errors are systematic rather than random. ARGUS abstains on 22/55
cells and, when it does answer, its errors are almost entirely one-directional:
28/33 answered cells are more severe than the adjudicated human gold label, while
none are less severe. Human gold contains only two high-risk cells, whereas ARGUS
assigns high risk to 24 cells. Because the pilot gold set contains very few high
labels, high precision and recall should be interpreted cautiously. The more
robust conclusion is that the current ARGUS assessor is over-sensitive to missing
or partial evidence and is better interpreted as a \emph{high-sensitivity
screening tool} for prioritizing human review.

\subsection{Calibration: from diagnosis to improvement}
\label{sec:calib}
The gold comparison also points to a fix. An error analysis of the
over-severe cells is sharp: of ARGUS's over-severe \emph{high} judgements,
$23/24$ have \texttt{evidence\_status=missing} and $20/24$ have
\texttt{retrieval\_quality=weak}. ARGUS treats ``weak retrieval found nothing'' as
a substantive \emph{high}, conflating a \emph{retrieval failure} with the paper
lacking the evidence, which the human, reading the full PDF, resolves to
low/medium. This is a fixable calibration target.

We add a deterministic calibration layer over the LLM output, keyed on the rich
fields it already produces: the dominant rule converts a weak-retrieval
\emph{high} (no contradiction signalled in the rationale) either to \emph{medium}
(keep coverage) or to \texttt{unknown} (abstain); smaller rules soften a few
anachronistic or reporting-gap \emph{high}s. Against the gold
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}), both policies cut over-severity and preserve high-risk
recall ($0.50$): severity falls without losing detection. The keep-coverage
policy keeps all 33 answers and raises exact agreement from $0.12$ to $0.46$; the
abstain policy reaches the highest weighted $\kappa$ ($0.25$) but collapses
coverage to 13 answered cells. Neither addresses the root cause, weak retrieval,
so the lift that would add \emph{correct} coverage must come from better evidence
grounding. The human gold thus does more than evaluate ARGUS; it calibrates it.

Figure~\ref{fig:calib_tradeoff} visualizes the trade-off behind
Table~\ref{tab:calib}. The demotion policy preserves coverage, keeping 33
answered cells, while reducing over-severe judgments from 29 to 18 and improving
exact agreement. The abstention policy is more conservative: it reduces
over-severe judgments to 6 and improves weighted $\kappa$ to 0.25, but leaves
only 13 answered cells. Thus, calibration can convert erroneous high-risk
judgments into either medium-risk judgments or honest abstentions, but it cannot
by itself recover the missing evidence needed for correct coverage.
