\section{Discussion and Research Plan}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
The present results are deliberately preliminary. They use a small number of
synthetic fixtures rather than a real corpus, and the baseline is an intentional
lower bound, so its low detection rate should be read as a calibrated starting
point, not as evidence about the eventual system. More fundamentally, flaw
injection certifies an auditor against the \emph{injected} threat on a single
dimension; it does not certify the absence of other, un-injected threats, and it
inherits whatever blind spots the flaw taxonomy has. We therefore treat detection
and localization as necessary, not sufficient, evidence of an auditor's quality,
and pair them with the false-alarm rate to guard against a system that flags
everything.

\paragraph{Construct validity: what ARGUS measures.}
A measurement is only as good as the link between the construct and the task that
operationalizes it~\cite{jacobs2021measurement,raji2021benchmark}. ARGUS audits
whether a paper's identification assumptions are \emph{adequately supported by its
reported evidence}---reported-evidence adequacy is an operational proxy for
identification credibility, not causal validity itself. The two can diverge: a
study may report every diagnostic yet still be invalid, or omit a check yet be
sound. ARGUS therefore deliberately \emph{flags rather than judges}, and a further
gap separates the construct from the task---retrieving the relevant evidence. Our
real-paper runs show this is the binding constraint: a reasoning assessor handed
off-target evidence over-flags every dimension, so ARGUS reports a
\emph{retrieval-quality} status and an explicit \texttt{unknown} risk
(``the system could not find the evidence'') distinct from a substantive
high-risk judgement (``the paper lacks it''). The central methodological finding
is thus that moving from synthetic fixtures to real papers is bottlenecked not by
causal reasoning but by \emph{evidence grounding}, which is why ARGUS separates
retrieval, relevance filtering, and adequacy assessment.

\paragraph{Research plan.}
The doctoral programme builds on the result in \S\ref{sec:results}, in four stages.
\emph{(1)~Harden the reasoning assessor.} The adequacy-reasoning policy already
recovers detection on the synthetic fixtures; the next step is to stress it on
harder, subtler flaws and to study its calibration, robustness across models, and
the traceability of its cited evidence---under the same flaw-injection protocol, so
gains stay attributable and reproducible.
\emph{(2)~Dimension-level expert annotation.} We already audit a real corpus of
published DID papers (\S\ref{sec:realpapers}); the missing piece is
\emph{dimension-level} expert labels of identification risk---the existing
corpus is labelled at the method/claim level, not per assumption. Collecting
these (with multiple annotators, recording disagreement) would calibrate whether
ARGUS's high/medium/\texttt{unknown} judgements match expert opinion and where
experts themselves disagree.
\emph{(3)~Human-expert studies.} Measure whether ARGUS reports actually help
domain experts---calibration of trust, time saved, and errors avoided---since the
system is explicitly designed to assist, not replace, expert judgement.
\emph{(4)~Broader policy domains.} Extend beyond the China emissions-trading case
to other environmental and, eventually, general empirical-economics settings,
testing how far the rubric-plus-injection methodology generalizes.
