\begin{abstract}
Empirical economics leans heavily on difference-in-differences (DID) designs,
and nowhere more visibly than in environmental policy, our motivating case. The
credibility of these studies rests on identification assumptions that are often
asserted rather than audited. We present \textbf{ARGUS}, a responsible-AI
framework that audits the \emph{identification credibility} of such studies
instead of adjudicating the true causal effect, which is never observed. ARGUS
decomposes identification into auditable dimensions, gathers in-paper evidence,
assesses each assumption--implication--evidence chain, and localizes risk in a
transparent, human-reviewable report. We validate the auditor with synthetic flaw
injection, which supplies reproducible local ground truth without the true effect.
Once we remove the leakage that made naive evaluation circular, a deterministic
keyword baseline detects only $0.182$ of injected flaws and cannot see
flawed-but-present evidence; swapping only the assessment policy for a model that
judges evidence \emph{adequacy} raises detection to $1.000$ with no false alarms.
Evidence presence, in other words, is not evidence adequacy. On 27 real published
DID papers the binding constraint shifts to evidence grounding: the framework
reports retrieval failure rather than over-flagging, and localizes where
real-paper auditing is hard. We also build a pilot human-gold calibration set,
where expert disagreement is mostly adjacent and partly driven by design
applicability. Comparing ARGUS to the adjudicated gold exposes a systematic
over-severity bias; a lightweight, anchor-based calibration layer reduces it while
preserving high-risk recall. ARGUS is thus a high-sensitivity screening tool that
flags dimensions for expert review rather than issuing verdicts. The human-gold
study is pilot-scale.

\end{abstract}
